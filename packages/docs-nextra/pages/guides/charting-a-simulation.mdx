# Charting a Simulation

This guide is a set of recipes for turning a random sample into a chart a reader can watch
change: counting draws into categories, binning them into intervals, summarizing a sample,
and comparing two samples side by side. It assumes you already know how to write a
`<chart>{:dn}` — see the [`<chart>{:dn}` reference](../reference/chart) for its attributes
and properties.

<Callout type="info">
Every number a chart below draws can be read back: either from the `<tally>{:dn}` or
`<binCounts>{:dn}` that produced it, or from the chart itself — `$c.binCounts{:dn}`,
`$series.median{:dn}`. So a sentence, a table or an `<answer>{:dn}` beside the picture can
say what the picture shows, and stay right when the reader resamples.
</Callout>

---

## Count draws into named categories

[`<tally>{:dn}`](../reference/tally) counts how many values fall into each category, and
`<chart>{:dn}` draws the counts. The pieces are: draw the sample, map each draw onto a
category, count, chart.

```doenet-editor-horiz
<setup>
  <numberList name="pop">30 45 12 60</numberList>
  <textList name="labels">North South East West</textList>
  <cumulativeSum name="cum">$pop</cumulativeSum>
  <number name="total"><sum>$pop</sum></number>
  <sampleRandomNumbers name="draws" type="discreteUniform"
    from="1" to="$total" numSamples="60" />
  <searchSorted name="which" target="$draws">$cum</searchSorted>
  <tally name="counts" categories="1 2 3 4">$which</tally>
</setup>

<chart type="bar" categories="$labels">
  <shortDescription>Where 60 sampled individuals came from</shortDescription>
  <yLabel>count</yLabel>
  $counts
</chart>

<callAction actionName="resample" target="$draws">
  <label>Draw again</label>
</callAction>
```

Five tags hold the whole simulation:
[`<sampleRandomNumbers>{:dn}`](../reference/sampleRandomNumbers) draws the sample,
[`<cumulativeSum>{:dn}`](../reference/cumulativeSum) accumulates the subpopulation sizes,
[`<searchSorted>{:dn}`](../reference/searchSorted) maps every draw onto the subpopulation it
landed in, [`<tally>{:dn}`](../reference/tally) counts them, and `<chart>{:dn}` draws the
counts.

Give `<tally>{:dn}` its categories rather than leaving it to discover them. A subpopulation
that draws nothing then still counts zero and keeps its bar, instead of dropping out and
shifting the others along — which would make the chart jump every time the reader resamples.

`<callAction>{:dn}` redraws the sample, and everything downstream follows.

## Bin a continuous sample into intervals

For a measurement rather than a category, `type="histogram"{:dn}` bins the sample itself:
its `bins` are the cut points, and each bar is as tall as the number of draws that fall
between one pair of them.

```doenet-editor-horiz
<setup>
  <sampleRandomNumbers name="z" type="gaussian" mean="0" variance="1"
    numSamples="200" />
</setup>

<chart name="h" type="histogram" bins="-4 -3 -2 -1 0 1 2 3 4">
  <shortDescription>200 draws from a standard normal distribution</shortDescription>
  <yLabel>count</yLabel>
  $z
</chart>

<p>Counts: $h.binCounts</p>

<callAction actionName="resample" target="$z">
  <label>Draw again</label>
</callAction>
```

Write the cut points out rather than leaving them to the chart, and give them room: a bin
the sample sometimes reaches and sometimes does not still keeps its place, so the picture
stands still while the reader resamples and only the bars move. Leave `bins` off and the
chart chooses cut points from the sample it has, which is what you want for a fixed set of
data and not for one that is redrawn.

`$h.binCounts{:dn}` is what the bars are, and `$h.binEdges{:dn}` the cut points, so the
histogram does the counting *and* hands the counts back.
[`<binCounts>{:dn}`](../reference/binCounts) counts the same way when you want the numbers
without a picture — in a table, or as the values of a bar chart whose bars you want named
by something other than their cut points.

## Compare two samples in one chart

Give the chart a `<series>{:dn}` per sample. Each is counted separately and drawn in its own
color, and its `<label>{:dn}` names it in the legend.

```doenet-editor-horiz
<setup>
  <numberList name="edges">-3 -2 -1 0 1 2 3</numberList>
  <textList name="bins">-2.5 -1.5 -0.5 0.5 1.5 2.5</textList>
  <sampleRandomNumbers name="a" type="gaussian" mean="0" variance="1"
    numSamples="200" />
  <sampleRandomNumbers name="b" type="gaussian" mean="1" variance="1"
    numSamples="200" />
  <binCounts name="ha" bins="$edges">$a</binCounts>
  <binCounts name="hb" bins="$edges">$b</binCounts>
</setup>

<chart type="bar" categories="$bins">
  <title>Two samples, means 0 and 1</title>
  <shortDescription>200 draws from each of two normal distributions</shortDescription>
  <yLabel>count</yLabel>
  <series><label>mean 0</label>$ha</series>
  <series><label>mean 1</label>$hb</series>
</chart>

<callAction actionName="resample" target="$a">
  <label>Draw the first again</label>
</callAction>
<callAction actionName="resample" target="$b">
  <label>Draw the second again</label>
</callAction>
```

Both series are binned against the same cut points, which is what makes the bars comparable:
a bar in one series stands next to the bar for the same interval in the other. Counting the
samples separately and drawing bars is what puts two of them in one picture — a histogram
bins and draws one sample, so two of them would be two charts.

Use `layout="stacked"{:dn}` instead when the two samples make up a whole and the total is
what a reader should read.

## Compare two samples by their summaries

A box plot asks a different question of a sample than a histogram does. A histogram shows the
shape of the distribution; a box plot reduces it to five numbers and draws those — one box
per `<series>{:dn}`, running from the first quartile to the third with the median across it —
so two samples side by side are compared by where their middles and their spreads sit.

```doenet-editor-horiz
<setup>
  <sampleRandomNumbers name="a" type="gaussian" mean="70" variance="25"
    numSamples="40" />
  <sampleRandomNumbers name="b" type="gaussian" mean="75" variance="25"
    numSamples="40" />
</setup>

<chart type="box">
  <title>Two samples, means 70 and 75</title>
  <shortDescription>Forty draws from each of two normal distributions</shortDescription>
  <yLabel>score</yLabel>
  <series name="control"><label>control</label>$a</series>
  <series name="treated"><label>treated</label>$b</series>
</chart>

<p>Medians: $control.median and $treated.median. The treated sample's middle half ran from
$treated.quartile1 to $treated.quartile3.</p>

<callAction actionName="resample" target="$a">
  <label>Draw the first again</label>
</callAction>
<callAction actionName="resample" target="$b">
  <label>Draw the second again</label>
</callAction>
```

Naming each `<series>{:dn}` is what makes its summary readable: `minimum`, `quartile1`,
`median`, `quartile3`, `maximum` and `outliers` are all reported per series, so the sentence
under the chart is drawn from the same numbers the boxes are. Resampling moves both.

## Chart two sampled variables against each other

A scatter plot needs a pair of numbers per point: the series holds the vertical coordinates
and its `x` the horizontal ones. Sample one variable, derive the other from it, and the
relationship between them is what the reader sees.

```doenet-editor-horiz
<setup>
  <sampleRandomNumbers name="height" type="uniform" from="150" to="190"
    numSamples="40" />
  <sampleRandomNumbers name="noise" type="gaussian" mean="0" variance="16"
    numSamples="40" />
  <numberList name="weight">
    <repeat for="$height" valueName="h" indexName="i">
      <number>0.9 * $h - 90 + $noise[$i]</number>
    </repeat>
  </numberList>
</setup>

<chart type="scatter">
  <shortDescription>Weight against height for forty sampled people</shortDescription>
  <xLabel>height (cm)</xLabel>
  <yLabel>weight (kg)</yLabel>
  <series x="$height">$weight</series>
</chart>

<callAction actionName="resample" target="$height">
  <label>Draw again</label>
</callAction>
```

[`<repeat>{:dn}`](../reference/repeat) is what pairs the two: it walks the sampled heights,
and `$i{:dn}` reaches the draw of noise that belongs with each. Both lists are the same
length, so every point has both of its coordinates.

## Ask about what the chart shows

Because the counts are numbers the document can read, the same numbers that drew the chart
can be asked about.

```doenet-editor-horiz
<setup>
  <sampleRandomNumbers name="z" type="gaussian" mean="0" variance="1"
    numSamples="200" />
</setup>

<chart name="c" type="histogram" bins="-4 -3 -2 -1 0 1 2 3 4" size="small"
  displayValues>
  <shortDescription>200 draws from a standard normal distribution</shortDescription>
  <yLabel>count</yLabel>
  $z
</chart>

<answer>
  <label>How many of the 200 draws landed between -1 and 1?</label>
  <number><sum>$c.binCounts[4] $c.binCounts[5]</sum></number>
</answer>

<p>The tallest bar holds <number><max>$c.binCounts</max></number> draws.</p>

<callAction actionName="resample" target="$z">
  <label>Draw again</label>
</callAction>
```

`$c.binCounts{:dn}` is one number per bar, in the order the bars are drawn, so the answer
and the sentence are both computed from the picture and stay right when the reader
resamples. `$c.values{:dn}` is the sample itself, for a summary the bins do not answer.
