# `<barChart>{:dn}`

<ComponentDisplay name='barChart'/>

`<barChart>{:dn}` draws one bar per number it is given. Pair it with
[`<tally>{:dn}`](tally) or [`<binCounts>{:dn}`](binCounts) and a sampling simulation is
a handful of components, however large the sample.

Bare numbers work as well as `<number>{:dn}` children, so `<barChart>41 63 18</barChart>`
draws three bars. They are read the way [`<sum>{:dn}`](sum) reads them, so an expression
such as `1/2` or `2^3` gives a height too.

Name the bars with `categories`; leave it out and they are numbered 1, 2, 3. A category
is a label and nothing else — the bars are evenly spaced whatever it says, so
`categories="1 5 6"` writes 1, 5, 6 under three equally spaced bars rather than placing
them at those positions. Numbers name bars as readily as words do, including a
[`<tally>{:dn}`](tally)'s own `.categories`.

The categories are matched to the bars in order, and there is one category per bar: a
category with no bar under it is dropped, and a bar with no category of its own keeps its
number. So `categories` given a list that is not the same length as the values — a
[`<binCounts>{:dn}`](binCounts)'s `.binEdges`, which holds one more cut point than there
are bins — labels what it can and leaves the rest as they were.

The vertical axis scales itself to the data, unless you set `yMin` or `yMax` yourself —
see [The vertical scale](#the-vertical-scale) below.

## What it is drawn with

`<barChart>{:dn}` renders through **PreFigure**, not the graph renderer, which buys
three things:

- **Category names on the axis.** `<graph>{:dn}` has no custom tick support, so the axis
  of a chart built from `<repeat>{:dn}` and `<rectangle>{:dn}` inside a graph can only
  number its bars; naming them means placing every name yourself as a second row of
  anchored labels.
- **One drawing however many bars.** The whole chart compiles to a single image, so no
  bar needs a graphical component and a renderer of its own — which is what drawing a
  chart with [`<repeat>{:dn}`](repeat) and [`<rectangle>{:dn}`](rectangle) costs. The
  values themselves are still components, one per bar; what the single drawing saves is
  everything downstream of them.
- **A chart a screen reader can walk.** Every bar carries its category and value as an
  annotation, so the chart is navigable rather than merely present. Give it a
  `<shortDescription>{:dn}` and that becomes the description of the figure as a whole.

The cost is that the PreFigure runtime is fetched the first time a page uses it, so the
first chart on a page takes a moment to appear. Pages with no chart on them fetch
nothing.

## The vertical scale

Left alone, the axis runs from zero up to the first tick past the tallest bar, so the
tallest bar never touches the top of the frame. The ticks are evenly spaced by a round
number — 1, 2 or 5 times a power of ten — and they are counted from zero, the baseline
the bars are measured against, rather than from the bottom of the box.

A chart of whole numbers is never labeled in fractions, and a chart of fractions is: a
chart of counts is labeled 1, 2, 3 and not 0.5, 1, 1.5, while a chart of proportions
gets ticks inside the unit interval rather than only at its two ends. This holds while
the axis' own ends are whole numbers too — set `yMin` or `yMax` to a fraction and the
labels may be fractions, since a whole step could not reach the bounds you asked for.

A negative value hangs its bar below the axis, and the axis drops to the first tick past
the smallest value. With `displayValues`, that bar's number is printed *below* it, at
the end of the bar rather than up on the axis.

A value that is not a finite number — a symbolic `<math>{:dn}`, or a `<number>{:dn}`
whose content does not parse — gets no bar at all, and is reported as a warning. Its
place on the axis is kept, so the remaining bars stay under their own categories, but it
is left empty rather than drawn as a zero, which is a value the chart does not have.

`yMin` and `yMax` replace the automatic bounds, and either may be set on its own — give
only `yMax` and the bottom of the axis is still chosen from the data. What they are
checked as is the resulting pair: if either bound is not a finite number, or `yMin` is
not below `yMax`, *both* are discarded and the chart scales itself as if neither had been
given, since a box of zero or negative height has nothing to draw in. Bars are always
measured from zero, so setting `yMin` above zero cuts them off at the bottom of the frame
and their drawn heights no longer compare the way their values do — and a range that
leaves out zero entirely, such as `yMin="100" yMax="200"` over a bar of height 4, draws
an empty frame.

The `yMin` and `yMax` *properties* report the scale the chart was drawn at, whether it
came from the attributes or from the data, so `$chart.yMax` is the top of the axis you
can see.

An empty chart still draws a box one tick tall rather than collapsing, and a value of
zero keeps its slot and its category label rather than being skipped.

## Size and spacing

`size` — or an explicit `width` — sets how wide the chart is, and `aspectRatio` sets its
width divided by its height, 1.5 by default. An `aspectRatio` that is not a positive
number is ignored and 1.5 is used, as is one so small that the height it asks for is
larger than a number can represent. A very large one is honored as written, so
`aspectRatio="1000"` really does draw a chart a thousand times wider than it is tall.

`barWidth` is how much of its one-unit slot each bar fills, from just above 0 up to 1;
the rest is the gap to the next bar. A value outside that range is reported as a warning
and 0.8 is used instead. Like `aspectRatio`, `yMin` and `yMax`, the `barWidth` property
reads back the number the chart was drawn with rather than the one that was asked for.

Category names are drawn side by side under the bars and nothing moves them out of each
other's way, so they have to fit in the room a bar gets: the chart's width divided by
roughly the number of bars. Six six-character names under a `size="small"` chart do not
fit, and run together into an unreadable line. Widen the chart, shorten the names, or use
fewer bars — a histogram's bins, for instance, read as well labeled by their midpoints as
by their ranges, in half the width.

The room reserved to the left of the chart is sized for the widest number the vertical
axis carries, so a chart counting in the thousands is not clipped where one counting in
tens would have been. That width is estimated rather than measured — the text is laid
out when the chart is compiled, after the space for it has been chosen — so a chart with
unusually wide axis numbers may still sit closer to its left edge than one with narrow
ones.

## Examples

### Example: a chart of counts


```doenet-editor-horiz
<barChart categories="North South East West" displayValues>
  <shortDescription>Population by region</shortDescription>
  <yLabel>people</yLabel>
  <number>41</number>
  <number>63</number>
  <number>18</number>
  <number>78</number>
</barChart>
```



`displayValues` prints each bar's value above it.



---

### Example: charting a simulation


```doenet-editor-horiz
<setup>
  <numberList name="pop">30 45 12 60</numberList>
  <textList name="labels">North South East West</textList>
  <cumulativeSum name="cum">$pop</cumulativeSum>
  <number name="total"><sum>$pop</sum></number>
</setup>

<sampleRandomNumbers name="draws" type="discreteUniform"
  from="1" to="$total" numSamples="60" hide />
<searchSorted name="which" target="$draws" hide>$cum</searchSorted>
<tally name="counts" categories="1 2 3 4" hide>$which</tally>

<barChart categories="$labels">
  <shortDescription>Where 60 sampled individuals came from</shortDescription>
  <yLabel>count</yLabel>
  $counts
</barChart>

<callAction actionName="resample" target="$draws">
  <label>Draw again</label>
</callAction>
```



Five tags hold the whole simulation, however large `numSamples` is:
[`<sampleRandomNumbers>{:dn}`](sampleRandomNumbers) draws the sample,
[`<cumulativeSum>{:dn}`](cumulativeSum) accumulates the subpopulation sizes,
[`<searchSorted>{:dn}`](searchSorted) maps every draw onto the subpopulation it landed
in, [`<tally>{:dn}`](tally) counts them, and `<barChart>{:dn}` draws the counts. The
*markup* is what stays fixed: raising `numSamples` still adds a component per draw and
another per subpopulation index, since each of those is a value of its own. What does
not grow is what you write, or the number of bars.

Drawing again redraws the chart. Because `<tally>{:dn}` is given its categories rather
than left to discover them, a subpopulation that happens to get no draws still counts
zero, and the chart keeps that bar — at height zero — instead of dropping it and
shifting the others along.

### Example: a histogram of a continuous quantity


```doenet-editor-horiz
<setup>
  <numberList name="edges">-3 -2 -1 0 1 2 3</numberList>
  <textList name="bins">-2.5 -1.5 -0.5 0.5 1.5 2.5</textList>
</setup>

<sampleRandomNumbers name="z" type="gaussian" mean="0" variance="1"
  numSamples="200" hide />
<binCounts name="h" bins="$edges" hide>$z</binCounts>

<barChart categories="$bins">
  <shortDescription>200 draws from a standard normal distribution</shortDescription>
  <yLabel>count</yLabel>
  $h
</barChart>

<callAction actionName="resample" target="$z">
  <label>Draw again</label>
</callAction>
```



[`<binCounts>{:dn}`](binCounts) turns the sample into counts per interval and
`<barChart>{:dn}` draws them. Drawing again a few times shows the shape settling toward
the bell it is drawn from — and how much a sample of 200 still wobbles.

Each bin is named by its midpoint rather than by its range. `-3 to -2` says more, but six
labels of that length do not fit side by side under six bars: they run into one another
and the axis becomes unreadable. See [Size and spacing](#size-and-spacing).

### Example: reading the chart's own numbers back


```doenet-editor-horiz
<barChart name="chart" categories="A B C" size="small">
  <shortDescription>Three values</shortDescription>
  <number>4</number>
  <number>9</number>
  <number>2</number>
</barChart>

<p>Values: $chart.barValues</p>
<p>Categories: $chart.categories</p>
<p>Total: <sum>$chart.barValues</sum></p>
<p>The axis runs from $chart.yMin to $chart.yMax.</p>
```



The chart's values, category names and axis bounds are readable properties, so a
sentence can quote the same numbers the picture shows without repeating them in the
source. Nothing above sets `yMax`, so the chart chose it — and reading it back gives the
value the axis was actually drawn to.
